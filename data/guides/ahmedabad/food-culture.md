## A mostly vegetarian cuisine

Gujarat is mainly vegetarian, even though it has a long coastline. Several communities, including Muslim and Parsi communities and some coastal groups, do eat fish, chicken or mutton.

## The everyday meal and the thali

The everyday Gujarati meal is often summed up as dal-bhat-rotli-shaak:

- dal (lentils) or kadhi, made from buttermilk and gram flour;
- bhat (rice);
- rotli (flatbread);
- shaak (vegetable dishes, which can be spicy or sweet).

A full thali adds:

- a dish of whole pulses (kathor);
- one or more farsan, savoury side dishes or snacks;
- a sweet (mishthaan).

A festive thali can hold more than a dozen items. Certain dishes are traditionally served together: kadhi comes with a lentil preparation, for example, and raita is not served in the same meal. Buttermilk (chaas) is the usual drink on the side.

## Sweet, salty and sour together

Many Gujarati dishes balance sweet, salty and spicy flavours. Sugar or jaggery is often added to vegetables and dal, which softens the spice. Lemon, lime, tomato, tamarind and kokum bring sourness, and salt, sugar and citrus are used often, partly to help prevent dehydration in the region's intense heat.

## Farsan and snacks

Farsan are savoury dishes eaten with the meal or as snacks on their own. Common ones include:

- **dhokla**, a soft steamed cake;
- **khandvi**, rolls of gram flour and yogurt topped with mustard seed, coriander and coconut;
- **fafda**;
- **handvo**, a savoury steamed cake of rice flour, beans, yogurt and bottle gourd;
- **thepla**, a mildly spiced flatbread, often with vegetables in the dough;
- **khakhra**.

Jalebi is a common sweet.

## Food through the year

Gujarati cooking follows the seasons:

- In summer, when mangoes are plentiful, fresh mango pulp (keri no ras) often joins the meal, and warming spices are used less.
- Undhiyu, a winter dish of mixed vegetables traditionally cooked upside down in clay pots, is eaten by most Gujarati families at least once a year at Makar Sankranti in January.
- Dry sweets such as magas and ghooghra are made for weddings and Diwali.

Fasting is common, and on fasting days some people eat only milk, dried fruit and nuts.

## Regional styles

Gujarati cooking varies across five regions: North Gujarat, Kathiawad, Kachchh, Central Gujarat and South Gujarat. The food of Surat, Kutch, Kathiawad and North Gujarat is the most distinctive. In Saurashtra villages, winter meals feature thick millet rotla and wheat bhakri with garlic chutney, onion and chaas.
