## Before you travel

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) advises travellers to India to:

- be up to date with routine vaccinations;
- discuss hepatitis A, hepatitis B and typhoid vaccines with a health professional before the trip;
- strongly consider travel health insurance that includes medical evacuation.

Typhoid in South Asia is often drug-resistant. Depending on where you are arriving from, India may ask for proof of polio or yellow fever vaccination, so check the current entry rules.

## Food and water

Travellers' diarrhoea is common in India: more than half of visitors on a two-week trip may get it. To lower the risk:

- drink only boiled, bottled or filtered water;
- eat food that is fully cooked and served hot;
- wash your hands often;
- take care with street food;
- avoid contact with fresh water, especially after heavy rain or flooding.

Hepatitis E is also widespread and can cause serious liver illness; it is especially dangerous in pregnancy.

## Mosquitoes

Dengue occurs across most of India, and the risk is highest in the wet season, including the monsoon months of June to September. The mosquitoes that carry it bite both indoors and outdoors. Chikungunya, which causes long-lasting joint pain, also has outbreaks, and malaria is present throughout the country; malaria mosquitoes bite mostly between dusk and dawn. The CDC also advises insect repellent, and long sleeves and trousers in areas with vegetation.

## Heat and air quality

Heat illness is a real risk in the hot season. The CDC advises you to:

- drink and eat regularly;
- wear light, loose clothing;
- limit physical activity when temperatures are high.

Air pollution is a serious problem in Indian cities. Check the daily air quality, and cut back outdoor activity on bad days, particularly if you are older, are travelling with children, or have asthma, lung disease or heart disease.

## Animals

India has the highest rabies burden in the world, and rabid dogs are common. Monkeys at temples and tourist sites can be aggressive: do not carry food on you near them, and do not touch or feed any animal. Get medical care at once after any animal bite.

## Medical care

Large private hospitals in big cities generally meet high international standards, but quality elsewhere varies. Hospitals in big cities usually accept major credit cards, while smaller facilities may take only cash. Hotel staff can help you find a doctor.
